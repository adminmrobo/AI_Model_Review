Time Series & Stock Prices — Beat the Baseline First

To feed stock prices into a neural network, you first cut them into windows, normalize them so no future information leaks in, and decide what to predict (the target). In this part, using 506 trading days of real Apple stock prices, the simplest baseline "tomorrow = today", a linear model and an LSTM compete under the same method (walk-forward). The results are reported as they are, good or bad. This is an educational example, not investment advice.

1Windows & Targets

Windows, normalization, targets — turning a row of numbers into a problem

A single row of prices is not enough to learn from. You have to turn it into a problem: "look at the last L days and predict the next day." Each time you slide the window by one step, you get one more (input, answer) pair.

Values shown
Window length

Why returns instead of prices, and how far normalization goes

Prices sit at different levels in different periods (about $120 in 2015, about $90 in 2016), so the test period brings ranges never seen during training. That is why we convert them to daily rates of change (log return = log(today/yesterday) × 100%). The reference for normalization (subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation) is computed only on the training period. If you use the mean of the whole period, information from the test period leaks into training (the leakage in Section 4). You also have to decide first what the target is: "next day's return (a number)", "up or down (direction)", or "size of the move".

2Hopes vs. Reality

Why deep learning raises hopes — and what the data says first

A neural network finds nonlinear relationships among many signals on its own. That is why people have high hopes for it on problems like stock prices, where many factors are tangled together. But there is something to ask the data first: "Is yesterday's return related to today's return?"

5Walk-Forward

Walk-forward validation — learn only from the past, step forward one step at a time

Evaluate exactly as in real use. Train only on the past up to that day, predict the next few days, and once those days have passed, add them in and train again. The test period must always come after the training period.

Analogies are a first step to understanding, but they can also seed misunderstanding. Fruit fly and brain analogies are marked by strength. Direct evidence that they actually do the same thing · Partial only some properties are similar · None no known counterpart

Check questions (click to expand the explanation)

Q1. With 505 returns and a 5-day window, how many (input, answer) pairs are there?

505 − 5 = 500.

Q2. If you retrain every 20 days over 200 test days, how many times do you train? How many pairs are in the first training run?

10 times. The first training run has 500 − 200 = 300 pairs, and the last has 480 pairs.

Q3. If a strategy traded 40 times and the transaction cost is 0.1% per trade, how much return was lost to costs?

40 × 0.1 = 4%p (approximate, based on log returns).

Q4. Picking the best of 100 random rules on the training period gave an excess of +43.7%p on the training period and −22.4%p on the test period. What does this show?

A result picked from many tries is fitted to chance, so it does not repeat on a new period (overfitting, selection bias). The test period should be used only once, at the end.

Q5. The baseline "tomorrow = today" had a smaller prediction error than the LSTM. How should this be written in a report?

Report the result as it is: "On this data (1 stock, 506 days, signal = past returns) and with this setup (20-day window, retraining every 50 days, 40 epochs), the LSTM did not beat the baseline (RMSE 1.462%·1.550% vs. 1.209%)." Then state which conditions could be changed and the limits of generalization.

How AI Works — One Stimulus, Four Paths

A dark shadow approaches. The human brain, machine learning, deep learning, and the fruit fly brain each process this scene along a different path. We run the four paths side by side, then pit machine learning, where people choose the features, against deep learning, which learns even the features by itself, on the same scenes. Finally, we separate the cases where computations resembling neural networks have been confirmed in real brains from the parts that remain estimates.

1Four paths

Four Paths — The Same Stimulus Flows at Once

A single stimulus enters four systems at the same time. They differ in the number of stages, what each stage does, and who decided what it does.

Solid box = established stage Dashed box = estimated / under research Rules set by people Stages learned from data Biological neural circuit Input / output
Stimulus
Path to inspect

Explained for beginners

All four paths share the same main flow: take in → organize → judge → act. The difference is "who decided what to use as clues (features)." In machine learning, people decide (yellow border); in deep learning, they are learned from data (green border). The fruit fly's looming-detection circuit is not learned but formed during development (pink border). You could say evolution "decided" it over a long time.

The human brain row shows only the main flow found in textbooks. The real brain has many feedback connections running backward, as well as fast shortcuts (such as the path from the eye to the superior colliculus in the midbrain). For the final "judge" stage, all that is known is that several regions work together, so it is drawn with dashed lines. The speed of the animation has nothing to do with actual processing time.

2Human brain

The Human Brain — Separating What Is Known from What Is Estimated

Each sense has its own receiving organ and its own route. Pick a sense, follow the path its signal takes, and check how firmly each stage is established.

Sense
What is known
  • Each sense has a different receiving organ and first pathway. Vision and hearing pass through the thalamus to the cerebral cortex. Smell skips the thalamus and goes from the olfactory bulb straight to the cortex [R10].
  • Neurons in the primary visual cortex respond strongly to bars or edges tilted at a particular orientation. This was first measured in cats [R3].
  • The monkey inferior temporal cortex has groups of neurons that respond selectively to faces [R4].
  • Learning changes the strength of synaptic connections.
Still estimated or under research
  • How the brain binds color, shape, motion, and sound, processed separately in different regions, into "a single object."
  • Exactly where, and by what computation, the decision "let's avoid it" is made. All that is known is that several regions are involved together.
  • Whether the brain learns by sending errors backward layer by layer, like backpropagation in deep learning. Hypotheses that the brain could do something similar by other means are being studied [R13].
  • The complete wiring diagram of the human brain. The fruit fly's whole-brain wiring diagram has been completed [R2], but the human brain has about 620,000 times as many neurons alone.

Why so many "estimates" for the human brain?

To measure the signals of individual neurons, electrodes must be placed in the brain. In humans this is possible only in very limited cases, such as patients who need surgery anyway. So much of our cell-level knowledge comes from animal experiments on cats, monkeys, and the like, while in humans we rely mainly on methods that measure activity over wide areas, such as brain imaging. In fruit flies, genetic tools can switch only the desired neurons on and off, so "this neuron is necessary for this behavior" can be tested directly. That is why this series uses the fruit fly as its guide.

3Machine learning

Machine Learning — People Design the Features

People: choose features → Machine: learn weightsSamuel, 1959

What people do

Looking at two 12×12 black-and-white scenes (before and after), the system must decide "is an object approaching?" First, people choose the clues for the decision, that is, the features. For example: "how much did the dark area grow?" and "how far did the shadow's center move?" The 288 pixels are reduced to 2 numbers.

What the machine does

It learns a weight for each of the 2 numbers plus a bias, 3 values in all, from 240 training examples (logistic regression). The line on the plane on the right is the result. After training, its skill is measured on 160 test scenes it has never seen.

Human-chosen features

Explained for beginners

Samuel's 1959 checkers program, which popularized the term "machine learning," worked this way too. People supplied a list of judgment criteria that "seemed relevant to the game," and the program, game after game, learned only how much weight to give each criterion [R11]. People decide what to look at; the machine decides how much to trust it.

So performance depends heavily on choosing good features. With feature A, a scene where "the whole room gets darker" is mistaken for an approaching object. This is not fixed even if such scenes are in the training data, because the information needed to tell them apart from approaching objects is already lost the moment everything is reduced to 2 numbers. It is solved only when a person imagines that situation and designs a new clue, as with feature B. This work of devising features is called feature engineering.

4Deep learning

Deep Learning — Learning Even the Features

288 pixels → 6 hidden neurons → probability of approach1,741 weights

What people do

People do not choose the features. Instead, they decide the shape of the neural network (how many hidden neurons, how many layers) and choose which scenes to show it, that is, the training data.

What the machine does

It takes the 288 pixels as they are. Each of the 6 hidden neurons learns, through its weights, "which cells changing in which way should turn it on." The small grid images in the middle figure are the result: features the neural network made by itself.

Example scene

Explained for beginners

Deep learning finds features in the data instead of having people choose them. So it can pick up clues people never thought of, and it is especially strong on data such as photos, sounds, and text, where features are hard to put into words. The neural network on this page is a very small model with just one hidden layer, but the principle is the same as in models with dozens of layers. As layers get deeper, the early layers learn simple features such as edges, and the later layers learn complex features that combine them [R12].

It is not free. Machine learning learns 3 weights; deep learning learns 1,741. The more there is to learn, the more examples are needed. When we actually ran Code 2, with only 8 training scenes the feature B approach reached 100% test accuracy while the pixel approach reached 93.9%; only at 240 scenes did the two become equal. And as "Without lighting changes" shows, deep learning also gets things wrong in situations that were not in the data. The human role of choosing features simply shifts to the role of choosing data.

Check questions (click to expand the explanation)

Q1. Using the word "feature," distinguish machine learning and deep learning in one sentence each.

In (traditional) machine learning, people choose the features and the machine learns a weight for each feature. In deep learning, the machine learns even the features from the raw data. Section 3 learned 3 weights; Section 4 learned 1,741.

Q2. Why does feature A in Section 3 get "Lights dimming" scenes wrong even though such scenes are in the training data?

The moment a scene is converted to feature A (area change, center shift), a lighting scene becomes a point where "the area grows a lot and the center stays put," landing on the same side as approaching scenes, even farther out. A single straight line cannot separate it from approaching scenes. Adding data does not fix it, because the features have lost the information.

Q3. Which human sense goes straight to the cerebral cortex without passing through the thalamus?

Smell. It goes olfactory epithelium → olfactory bulb → piriform cortex. Vision, hearing, and touch pass through the thalamus.

Q4. What do the fruit fly's LPLC2 and feature B in Section 3 have in common?

Both distinguish "an object expanding in all directions" from "simply getting darker." LPLC2 does not respond to dimming without motion. The difference is that feature B was designed by a person, while LPLC2 is a circuit formed during development.

Q5. What is wrong with the judgment "It scored 100% accuracy on the training data, so it will work well in the field too"?

It can fail when it meets situations not in the training data (the lighting changes in Section 4). You must measure it on unseen test data and check whether the situations that can occur in the field are included in the training data.
